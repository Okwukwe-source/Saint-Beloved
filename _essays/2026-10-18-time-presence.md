It is probably a fiction of my memory, but reality is consciousness, so my memory is reality, and I think my days were longer as a child. I remember playing games with my cousin on a Nokia keypad phone I had in primary school. Later, while still in primary school, I got my first smartphone: a Tecno P5. On it came my first forays into Subway Surfers and Winning Eleven. In secondary school, my dad got me a PSVita—a portable play station console, and on it I enjoyed Mortal Kombat and God of War I and 2. Life was good. I'd play these games with my cousin from morning unto the afternoon, perhaps take a break, and continue playing before I slept at night. Of course, my life didn't revolve around games only. I probably enjoyed more cartoons, movies, comics, and literature than the average child. My upbringing was unfettered and full of leisure (and it still is). In the idyllic childhood memories of my mind, I remember a distinct note of a lack of a sense of time. It seemed that I enjoyed life without care to the position of the hands of a clock and the corresponding position of the orbs of day and night. I simply lived in a space that felt eternal. Each day felt like it had no end, and when sleep rebutted that fantasy, the next day woke it again.

As I have grown older, I have had a more acute sense of time, and strangely, time feels faster. Or, rather, the sense of time moving faster makes me more acutely aware of time. I respond in likeness to the seeming speed of time, trying to rush my activities to catch up or surpass it; to do my devotions more quickly, to finish a book faster, to do every work and finish it as soon as I can, all because I want to finish “ahead of time” (a nonsense notion), or not let time slip by me without accomplishing what I want. That was me. Uncomfortable with the situation, I wanted to regain the “time of the toad” as Borges said in Avelino Arredondo; the sense of eternity I had as a child; the being in a moment without being conscious of time, which made the moment seem to have no end.

The lack of a sense of time didn't make moments seem eternal to me, rather, moments seemed eternal because I wasn't absent-minded. My attention to the games I played was volitional and focused, and that presence of mind is the key to eternity within time. Nothing is a better time and attention stealer than social media. The endless controversies of X and the bottomless pit of the content of Instagram and Tiktok is one in which when you get in the daytime, you come out in the night wondering how you lasted the whole day in it. This is because while social media hijacks your attention, which is why you lose awareness of time (or if you recognize its time-wasting capacity, become hyper-aware and anxious about your time slipping away while using it), one doesn't really pay attention to it (which is why it's a hijacking—that is, not conscious, volitional attention on your part). Reels and scrolling create a paradox in your mind: absent-minded attention. You are attentive so you lose sight of time, but you're not really attentive so you can't remember the reels prior to the one you're presently watching. The consciousness that social media influences in you, is one that steals eternity and gives a fleeting time as your reality. Compare this to when you're phone is dead and there's no electricity so you're forced to stay in your room doing nothing. In that state of boredom, of being forced to keep company with solitude, time doesn't seem to move. You can be acutely aware of time then, and yet, it'd seem endless, or, rather, static. Eternity is found in solitude, or, rather, in the attention of the mind to the present. Even if the mind is pulled by thoughts, the thoughts aren't exactly distractions since you're not doing anything else anyway. Even then, there is a sense of being present with your thoughts, and in that focused attention, whether volitional (like when reading a book or paying attention to a lecture), or in a forced solitude, you are present, and time dies.

Is this an injunction to delete your Instagram, Tiktok, X, and even YouTube? Perhaps. I've done precisely that myself. Maybe you should too. But I know the infinite mental stimulation you get from them probably has too strong a hold on you. So much so that you'd create the excuse that you use social media to consume valuable content, like educational content, as a basis for establishing legitimacy in your social media use. But as with the parable of the sower sowing the portion of the seeds on fertile land that grew but was choked with weeds, so is any valuable content you glean from social media choked up with the slop you almost certainly also consume, coupled with the loss of time, volitional attention, and capacity for silence and solitude. 

The smartphone itself is a problem. It is the most necessary technological tool to own today and yet, it is probably also the worst thing to happen to you. Perhaps we shouldn't blame the tool but blame the user. The hammer has no volition apart from the user anymore than the smartphone does. But you're not a specially-trained human. You are full of weakness and susceptible to the easiest of temptations. You will not transcend the algorithm of social media or the allure of mindless scrolling. App timers are probably nonsense as well. You'd breach the timers to consistently use the apps well beyond the set time because you found something worthwhile or fun to consume. Unfortunately for you, you need your smartphone.

The smartphone doesn't need to be deleted from your reality like social media does, but it certainly needs to be used consciously. The term “mindless scrolling” is an apt term for social media use. It's the absent-minded attention I was talking about; the act of using your phone or social media without being mindful or attentive or present as you use. You scroll which signifies you are somewhat conscious, but the scrolling, the consumption, is mindless, because you're not really there. It is this that steals time and dissipates the capacity for attention. The solution is to use your phone like a hammer. I remember a Kanye West tweet (ironic, a value I got from social media) where he said treat your phone like a hammer. You don't carry a hammer around for no reason and you certainly don't start touching it for no reason. Use your phone the exact way. You've been so used to using your phone in a mindless manner, that this will prove to be a difficulty, so much so that you'd probably give up if you tried and resume the mindless use. This essay would be forgotten the next minute after you desert the website. These things I write are more of injunctions to myself than to the reader. The “you” here is my ideal self telling my current self to do better. I'd certainly not give up, and I'd certainly do better and become more whole, more complete, as my eternal days, spent in the state of singular-minded attention to the present, go by. Hence, when it comes to this phone, I'd only pick it up when I've determine why I am doing so, and I'd do exactly that and nothing more with it (and one may say this intentional use can be applied to social media rather than a total deletion, but that's idealistic at best, and as I said, you will not transcend the algorithim. Don't fight difficult battles that are unnecessary). When I'm done using the phone for the exact purpose I intended to, I'd put down the phone and do nothing. 

The state of nothingness, of simply being idle and present to reality, may be difficult to fathom for some. Pascal said the whole problem of man is that he can't sit in his room quietly and do nothing. Your heart is restless, you start fidgeting when your phone dies, trying to find anything at all to do, rather than simply be still. But sitting still and being present may well be our purpose in life. When Martha was busy serving God and Mary sat still at His feet, gazing at Him or listening to Him with a full presence of mind, Martha was annoyed. But God told her that “one thing is needful” and that it was that which Mary was doing; that which “wouldn't be taken away from her”. In solitude, decorated with devotion, God is found. The noise of the world, from the phone and from social media, is nothing but a distraction from solitude, our ideal state, where communion with God, with Reality in its fullness, is found. Common communion to God in a religious gathering is the exception to the rule of finding God in solitude. But fundamentally, we are alone with God. 

The irony of social media, “social” being an adjective that signifies the media as a means of connecting people with each other, is the disconnect from people it brings. I remember the cliche’ of the family sitting at a table, each on their phones using social media and failing to be present and connect with each other. Social media is anti-social, and as people say “it isn't real life”. Tyler said in DOGTOOTH “I'm living life, y'all on the feed (yuck)”. There is a certain mutual exclusiveness between life and social media. The latter is more of virtual reality, entering a world of avatars and people stripped of personhood, and to be in it is to disconnect from the surrounding reality, which is life itself. In contrast to social media, solitude is living life; since in solitude, one confronts reality, but away from it, in the world of the screen, one is away from reality.
